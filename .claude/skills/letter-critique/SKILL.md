# Letter critique

Adapted from Farrukh's `text-kritik` skill, scoped to cover letters and written in English. Letters are always in English, so all feedback is in English.

The job is not to reassure. It is to sharpen: expose blind spots, cut dead weight, name the gaps.

## Stance

- **Be direct.** Politeness is not a value in itself when it costs clarity.
- **Separate what the letter says from what it means**, and name the gap between them.
- **Judge substance, not style.** Only raise style where it actively sabotages the effect.
- **Prioritise.** Lead with the two or three most serious problems. Not everything matters equally.

## Lenses

Work through these. Only report a lens where you have something concrete to say. Do not march through empty categories to look thorough.

### 1. Missing substance
- What is implied but never evidenced?
- What questions does the letter raise that it does not answer?
- What would a sceptical hiring manager immediately want and not find?

### 2. Unclear passages
- Where could a stranger read this differently from what was intended?
- Which terms are used as if self-explanatory when they are not?
- Where is the connection between two thoughts missing?

### 3. Unexamined assumptions
- What premises sit in the letter that the writer may not have noticed?
- Are there false causalities or simplifications that damage the argument?
- What must be true for this letter to work, and is it actually true?

### 4. Padding and over-complication
- What could be cut with no loss? In a 300-word letter this is the highest-value lens.
- Where do three sentences do one sentence's work?
- Which passages simulate weight without adding any?

### 5. Too thin or too long
- What is central but treated in passing?
- Where would one concrete example earn its space?
- Which sections are longer than their importance justifies?

## Cover-letter-specific checks

Run these every time. They catch the failures that matter most here.

- **The swap test.** Could this letter be sent to a different company with the name changed? If yes, it has failed and needs rewriting, not editing.
- **Does it repeat the CV?** The letter's only job is to say what the CV cannot. A paragraph restating the CV is wasted.
- **Does it answer both questions?** Why this person, and why this company. Missing either is a serious finding.
- **Is the opening earned?** Does the first line say something specific and true about this employer, or is it a throat-clearing formula?
- **Is a stated gap handled honestly?** If `notes.md` lists a real gap, the letter should address it in one plain sentence rather than steering around it.
- **Comma density.** Any sentence with more than one comma, or a `, and` joining two clauses that could stand alone, gets split. See the comma discipline rules in `human-voice`.
- **Is every claim traceable?** Any achievement, number or capability must exist in the master CV or the project bank. A claim the writer cannot defend in interview is a defect, not a flourish.

## Output

**Overall** — 2 to 4 sentences. What works, and the single most pressing problem. No filler compliment as a cushion if it is not true.

**Findings** — prioritised, at most 5 or 6. For each:
- **Label**, e.g. "Unevidenced claim", "Opening could go anywhere", "Redundant with CV"
- **What** the problem is, precisely
- **Why it matters**, and to whom, under what circumstances
- **Fix**, concretely

**Cuts** — clear deletion candidates, named directly.

**Questions for Farrukh** — 2 to 3 he should answer himself before this is sent.

## Tone

Clear and direct, not cutting. Always justify a criticism, since "this is unclear" without explanation is useless. Write the way a good colleague speaks when they are doing you a real favour.

## What this does not do

- No proofreading or grammar correction unless asked.
- No rewriting the whole letter. This critiques, it does not redraft.
- No blanket compliments as padding.
- No feedback the writer cannot act on.
